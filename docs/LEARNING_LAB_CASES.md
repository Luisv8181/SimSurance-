# Learning Lab: Initial Synthetic Cases

**Status:** Teaching prototypes only. These are not Pennsylvania reimbursement guidance, benefit determinations, or predictions. Names, amounts, and organizations are fictional. Do not substitute real rules until the source, version, effective date, section, and applicability have been verified.

## Case 1: The appointment that is not there

### Story
Jordan is a fictional 16-year-old who wants help with anxiety and has Medicaid coverage in this fictional scenario. A family member calls a provider listed in a directory, but the clinic says its next appointment is several weeks away.

### Learning objectives
- Explain why coverage does not guarantee an immediate appointment.
- Distinguish eligibility, covered services, network participation, and provider capacity.
- Identify what is known and what needs verification.

### Known facts
Jordan is described as covered in this scenario; a provider appears in a directory; the provider reports no immediate opening.

### Unknowns
Is the directory current? Does the provider participate in the member's specific plan for the needed service? Is the service covered in these circumstances? Does another appropriate provider have capacity? Do transportation, language, scheduling, or accessibility needs affect options?

### Walk-through
Coverage is one part of the route to care. A directory is like a map showing a possible destination; it does not guarantee that the destination is open, suitable, or reachable today. Verify plan/network details and explore appropriate access routes rather than concluding from one fact that the service is or is not covered.

### Discussion
1. Which part is an insurance question?
2. Which part is a provider-capacity question?
3. What information would help Jordan find an appropriate appointment?
4. How might transportation, language access, or accommodations change the journey?

**Model representation:** Learning narrative only. It does not adjudicate a claim or assert a specific Pennsylvania policy rule.

## Case 2: The $150 charge

A fictional clinic submits a claim with a billed charge of $150. For this exercise only, assume an invented contract rule says the allowed amount is $90 and the fictional member owes $0. Assume all required checks pass.

| Item | Illustrative amount |
|---|---:|
| Billed charge | $150 |
| Allowed amount under invented exercise rule | $90 |
| Fictional member responsibility | $0 |
| Fictional payer payment | $90 |
| Difference between billed charge and allowed amount | $60 |

Under these invented assumptions, the clinic receives $90 from the payer, not $150. The $60 difference is not automatically a bill to the member. Real outcomes depend on contract, benefit design, and applicable rules.

**Money-flow warning:** Do not add this service payment to a separate capitation payment as if both were separate costs of the same care. State the financing structure and accounting perspective before combining totals.

### Discussion
1. Why might a billed charge differ from the allowed amount?
2. Why is it unsafe to assume the member owes the difference?
3. Which source or contract section is needed before using these values for a real case?
4. How would the ledger change if the claim were unresolved rather than paid?

**Model representation:** Connect to the toy adjudicator and ledger only when inputs are explicitly illustrative. This is not evidence of current Pennsylvania rates or policy.

## Case 3: A budget is not a waiting room

A fictional behavioral-health organization has a hypothetical annual budget for defined services, but clinicians are fully booked and several positions are vacant.

### Learning objectives
- Distinguish financial resources from service capacity.
- Identify factors affecting appointment access beyond payment.
- Avoid assuming that a payment increase automatically creates immediate appointments.

### Walk-through
Money can support hiring, retention, supervision, space, and technology, but changes take time and depend on workforce availability and organizational decisions. The number of appointments available today is not identical to a budget line.

### Discussion
1. What could the organization do with additional resources?
2. What barriers might remain after a funding increase?
3. Which indicators would help evaluate whether access improved?
4. What unintended consequences could arise if payment incentives reward volume without considering quality or complexity?

**Model representation:** Qualitative scenario only. Any later numerical staffing or capacity assumptions must be labeled and justified separately.
