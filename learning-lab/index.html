Case file 01

Jordan can get coverage. Can Jordan get care?

Jordan is a fictional 16-year-old seeking help with anxiety. In this learning scenario, Jordan has Medicaid. A provider appears in the directory, but the clinic says the next opening is several weeks away.

What do we know?

Jordan is described as covered in the scenario; a provider appears in a directory; the provider reports no immediate opening.

What is still unknown?

Is the directory current? Does the provider participate in Jordan's specific plan for this service? Is the service covered in these circumstances? Are other appropriate providers available? Are language, transport, scheduling, or accessibility needs relevant?

What does the metaphor teach?

A provider directory is like a map to a possible destination. It does not guarantee the destination is open, suitable, or reachable today.

Case file 03 · Practice operations

Why does a private practice accept some insurance plans and not others?

A payer decision is a business, clinical-access, and contract decision. Practices usually weigh the likely benefit to clients against payment, eligibility requirements, workload, and risk. There is no single universal rule that every practice follows.

1. Client demand

Which plans do current and prospective clients have? Which communities does the practice aim to serve? Would joining the plan help people get access to appropriate care?

2. Net revenue, not just rate

Compare the contracted payment with clinician compensation, billing and collection time, documentation, denials, cancellations, payment delays, and other costs. The highest session rate may not yield the best net return.

3. Credentialing and eligibility

Check license and provider-type rules, credentialing, enrollment, malpractice requirements, group versus individual contracting, and whether the payer is accepting providers in that network.

4. Contract and administration

Review covered services, billing codes, authorizations, referrals, timely filing, audits, recoupments, appeals, documentation, and the resources needed to follow the rules.

5. Strategic fit

Consider referral flow, waitlists, clinician capacity, continuity, mission, payer concentration, and whether the contract supports a sustainable practice. A practice may apply, renew, decline, or reconsider participation subject to its contract and applicable law.

6. Recheck after joining

Track claims submitted and paid, denial reasons, days to payment, staff time per claim, completed visits, client access, and contract changes. Use aggregate, de-identified information for learning.

Source-backed guide · Pennsylvania

How does someone request behavioral-health services in Pennsylvania?

Pennsylvania's member guidance names the access route: the county mental-health office, then the county's behavioral-health managed-care organization. This lesson teaches only what the official page says — not which provider is in network or how long the wait is.

The request flow, as the state describes it

1. County mental-health office

Behavioral healthcare covered through Medicaid is overseen by the county's mental-health office. It helps determine what services and care a person qualifies for and connects them to its BH-MCO.

Question: Which county office serves this person?

2. BH-MCO assignment

Each county contracts with a BH-MCO for mental-health and drug-and-alcohol services. The BH-MCO is assigned based on county of residence.

Question: Which BH-MCO serves this county today?

3. Member handbook

The MCO sends a handbook outlining how to access services and the benefits available to the member.

Question: What does the current handbook say about this service?

4. Provider choice

The member has a choice of behavioral-health providers within the BH-MCO's network.

Question: Is this exact provider in network and available?

What Pennsylvania DHS says

“Your county will help determine what services and care you qualify for and will connect you to its behavioral health managed care organization.”

Source: Pennsylvania Department of Human Services, Request Behavioral HealthChoices Program Services . This describes the access route — not a specific person's enrollment, a provider's current network status, or appointment availability.

The page adds two practical notes. If you are enrolled in Medicaid for physical health, you also have access to mental-health and substance-use-disorder treatment through Behavioral HealthChoices. If you are not on Medicaid and have trouble paying, the county mental-health office may be able to help — contact them to learn about options.

How to apply and where to get help

The same official page lists the Medicaid application routes: online through COMPASS, in person at a county assistance office, by mail, or by phone (1-866-550-4355). The Customer Service Center is open Monday–Friday, 8:00 a.m.–4:30 p.m. For a crisis, call or text 988 — free, confidential, 24/7. If you email OMHSAS, the page warns not to include confidential details like a Social Security number or medical information.

None of these routes establishes a particular person's eligibility, benefit, or appointment. They are the documented starting points.

Federal context · Medicaid in schools

How can Medicaid pay for services delivered at school?

Some Medicaid-covered services can be delivered in school settings, but the school does not simply send Medicaid a bill and get paid. States use approved plan language, documentation rules, and claiming methods. This lesson teaches only what a federal resource index lists — not any specific rule that applies to a state.

Follow the money concepts

1. State plan authority

Medicaid payment for school-based services generally rests on approved state plan language or amendments (SPAs).

Question: Does the state plan describe these services and their payment method?

2. Documented services

Claims for services in schools must meet federal documentation requirements — for service claims, cost reporting, and time studies.

Question: What documentation does the claiming method require?

3. Administrative claiming

Some school staff time spent on Medicaid administrative activities can be claimed separately, often supported by time studies such as a Random Moment Time Study (RMTS).

Question: Which activities are administrative versus direct services?

4. Cost pools and cost reports

Some states use cost-based methods: costs are grouped into pools and reported, rather than paying a fixed rate per visit.

Question: What does the state's approved methodology define?

Federal context · managed-care financing

How are Medicaid managed-care rates set?

When a state pays a managed-care organization, it does not negotiate a price per visit. It pays a per-person rate that is supposed to cover a defined set of services over a defined period — and federal rules require those rates to be developed the way actuaries develop rates. This lesson teaches only what a federal CMS page states — not any specific rate for any plan, state, or service.

Four money concepts

1. Capitation

The managed-care regulation requires states to develop valid managed-care capitation rates: a prospective payment for a defined population, service scope, and period — paid to the MCO up front, not per visit.

Question: Is this payment per person, per period — or per service?

2. Actuarial soundness

Those rates must be developed in accordance with generally accepted actuarial principles and practices. "Actuarial soundness" is the federal quality bar: rates should reasonably reflect the expected costs of the covered services.

Question: What standard must the rate development meet?

3. Rate guides and rating periods

CMS publishes an annual Medicaid Managed Care Rate Development Guide. The current guide (released February 2026) applies to states setting rates for rating periods starting between July 1, 2026 and June 30, 2027.

Question: Which guide — and which rating period — applies to the analysis in front of you?

4. Rate certification

States document their rates in actuarial rate certifications, and CMS uses the guide as the basis for its review. The page is the index of the guides; the guides themselves are the underlying documents.

Question: Where would the actual rate calculation be documented and reviewed?
Read the source

What CMS says

“The managed care regulation requires that states develop valid managed care capitation rates in accordance with generally accepted actuarial principles and practices.”

Source: Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, Rate Review and Rate Guides . This is the federal standard, not a Pennsylvania rate, a BH-MCO contract rate, or a payment amount for any service.

Honest limits: this page is an index of federal rate-development guides, not the guides themselves. No rate for any specific plan, state, provider, or time period is stated here. Pennsylvania-specific rates or contract rules were not reviewed this run, and none have been encoded in the simulator.

Try it: who sets the rate?

A state Medicaid agency pays a managed-care plan a flat amount per member per month to cover behavioral-health services. Which description fits the federal requirement?

Capitation rates are prospective per-person payments with an actuarial development standard — not retrospective averages, and not amounts a clinic sets with a patient. The actual numbers for a given plan still require that plan's certified rates for the applicable rating period.

Case file 02

A budget isn't a waiting room.

A fictional behavioral-health organization has an annual budget for defined services, but clinicians are booked and several positions are vacant.

What might additional resources support?

Recruitment, retention, supervision, space, technology, or other operating needs. Each option takes time and depends on workforce availability and organizational decisions.

Why might access not improve immediately?

Funding and appointment capacity are different resources. Available qualified clinicians, schedules, geography, and the needs of the people seeking care all matter.

What should we measure?

For a real evaluation, define measures such as wait time, appointments offered, completed visits, continuity, workforce vacancies, and unmet need. A simulation should label assumptions and uncertainty rather than promise an outcome.

Federal context · coverage rules

Does my plan have to cover mental health the same as physical health?

A federal law called MHPAEA requires that when a plan covers mental health or substance use disorder (MH/SUD) benefits, the limits it places on those benefits cannot be less favorable than the limits on medical and surgical benefits. It does not require the plan to cover MH/SUD benefits in the first place. This lesson teaches only what the federal CMS page states — not any rule about any specific plan, and not legal advice.

Four parity concepts

1. Parity is equal treatment, not guaranteed coverage

MHPAEA "generally prevents" plans that provide MH/SUD benefits from imposing less favorable benefit limitations on them than on medical/surgical benefits — but it does not require plans to cover MH/SUD benefits at all. Separately, the Affordable Care Act requires MH/SUD services as one of ten essential health benefit categories in non-grandfathered individual and small group plans.

Question: If a plan covers no MH/SUD benefits, does MHPAEA force it to add them?

2. Quantitative vs. non-quantitative limits

Quantitative treatment limitations are numerical — visit limits, day limits. Non-quantitative treatment limitations (NQTLs) are process-based — pre-authorization, step therapy, medical management, network composition standards, and the methods used to set out-of-network reimbursement. Parity applies to both kinds, and a 2013 rule defines six benefit classifications where the comparison is made.

Question: Is a pre-authorization requirement quantitative or non-quantitative?

3. The predominant test, classification by classification

Financial requirements (copays, coinsurance) and treatment limitations on MH/SUD benefits cannot be more restrictive than the predominant requirements and limitations that apply to substantially all medical/surgical benefits in the same classification — inpatient in-network, inpatient out-of-network, outpatient in-network, outpatient out-of-network, emergency, or prescription drugs. Separate requirements that apply only to MH/SUD benefits are prohibited.

Question: Can you compare a plan's emergency-room copay with its outpatient therapy visit limit?

4. Prove it: comparative analyses and data

The 2024 final rules require plans and issuers to perform and document comparative analyses of how NQTLs are designed and applied, collect and evaluate data on material differences in access, and take reasonable action when the data show the NQTL is the cause. Discriminatory design — standards systematically disfavoring MH/SUD access — is prohibited. Plans must make their analyses available to federal or state authorities on request, and enrollees can request the information too.

Question: Who has to show that an NQTL is applied comparably — the enrollee or the plan?
Read the source

What CMS says

“The Paul Wellstone and Pete Domenici Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act of 2008 (MHPAEA) is a federal law that generally prevents group health plans and health insurance issuers that provide mental health or substance use disorder (MH/SUD) benefits from imposing less favorable benefit limitations on those benefits than on medical/surgical benefits.”

Source: Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, The Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act (MHPAEA) . This is the federal parity rule; it says nothing about any specific plan's coverage, cost sharing, or networks.

Honest limits: MHPAEA generally does not apply directly to small group plans (50 or fewer employees under ERISA/Code); some states have stricter requirements (not reviewed here). Medicaid MCOs, CHIP plans, and alternative benefit (benchmark) plans carry certain MHPAEA requirements through Social Security Act provisions — the Lab has not modeled them. Whether a particular plan complies is a plan-specific determination, not something this lesson can establish. This is educational information, not legal advice.

Try it: which rule applies?

A large-group plan covers both physical and mental-health benefits. Its plan requires pre-authorization for therapy visits but not for routine physical-therapy visits, and the plan has not shown a comparable, equally applied process for the medical/surgical side. Which statement best matches what the CMS page describes?

MHPAEA compares how limitations are designed and applied across benefit classifications — it does not require MH/SUD benefits to be offered, and it does not ban management outright. The plan must be able to show its work: a documented comparative analysis of the NQTL.

Small dictionary

Insurance words in human language.

Start here when a term appears in a lesson. These short definitions are educational; actual policy meanings must be checked in the relevant source.

Eligibility

Whether a person meets a program's requirements at a particular time. It does not automatically answer whether a particular service is authorized or a claim will be paid.

Provider network

The providers or organizations recognized by a plan under specified terms. A network listing does not guarantee an open appointment.

Claim

A structured request for payment for a service. A claim is not itself a payment and may need review.

Allowed amount

The amount recognized under applicable payment terms for a service. The calculation depends on the relevant contract, fee schedule, benefit, and circumstances.

Capitation

A prospective payment for a defined population, service scope, and period, under contract terms. It differs from paying a clinic for each individual service.

Access to care

The practical ability to find, reach, and receive appropriate care. Coverage matters, but so do capacity, transportation, language access, scheduling, and accommodations.
