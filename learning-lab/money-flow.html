Follow the money.

Mental-health financing can involve several organizations and many transactions. The important question is not only "how much money moved?" but also "what did this payment represent?"

Who does what?

Simplified example. Actual funding arrangements depend on the program, contract, service, and time period.

01 / PUBLIC FUNDING

Government

Provides program funding under applicable state and federal arrangements.

02 / PROGRAM ADMIN

Health plan

May receive a prospective payment for a defined population and covered scope.

↓ coordinates covered care
03 / CARE DELIVERY

Provider

Clinicians and organizations deliver covered services and submit claims or encounter records.

↑ may receive service payment
04 / CARE RECEIVED

Member

The person receives care when the service, provider, practical access, and applicable requirements align.

Careful: this is a conceptual map, not a complete flow of funds. A member does not necessarily send money directly to every actor. Contract structures vary.

Choose the transaction you are looking at

Same system, different questions. Choose a lens to learn what the transaction tells you and what it does not.

Prospective payment (often called capitation)

A plan may receive a set payment for a defined population and period, under contract terms. It is not the same transaction as a provider's claim for a visit.

Ask: which population, service scope, period, adjustments, and contract terms apply?

Do not infer member debt from subtraction alone. The difference between billed charge and allowed amount is not automatically the member's responsibility. Real results depend on applicable coverage, contracts, policy, and case facts.

The double-counting trap

Suppose a health plan receives a prospective payment, then pays a clinic for a service. If you add both amounts together and label that sum "the cost of the service," you may count two steps in the funding chain as though they were two separate final costs.

The correct treatment depends on the research question and accounting perspective. At minimum, name the entity whose spending you are measuring, the time period, and whether you are tracking transfers, service payments, administrative costs, or consolidated system expenditures.

Quick check

Questions to ask in any real analysis

  1. Who is the payer and who receives the payment?
  2. What does this transaction represent: funding transfer, prospective payment, claim payment, grant, or administrative expense?
  3. What period, population, services, and contract terms apply?
  4. Is this gross spending, a transfer between organizations, or consolidated system cost?
  5. Which source establishes the rule, and what is its effective date?
